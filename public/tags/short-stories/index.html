The Deep; it Beckons

The beach glows tonight. It’s a soft glow, a murmuration of light.
It’s not anything spectacular, but everything has that moonshine sheen to it. The moon hangs there in the sky, a quarter past her zenith, like some silver-glass fishing float adrift in a midnight sea.
On the shoreline an old fisherman heaves a roughened wooden rowboat towards the water. The keel of the boat gouges the sand as he drags it along, leaving behind a scar of shadow and silver. A few hours ago, the vessel had been bobbing in the surf with the pull of the moon and the will of the wind, knocking up against the knolls of beach grass some fifty yards back. Even if there had been a new moon, the summer squall that had overtaken the little cove would’ve been enough to push the tide line that high. The storm had been something fierce, cutting through the faulty shutters of the fisherman’s cottage, but the tumult had suddenly died, leaving the little inlet as still as could be. With a sudden slake give, the boat reaches the waterline, and the fisherman propels her forward, slotting right into her belly like he has done a thousand times before.
The water’s still tonight, even for a sudden clearing. It’s eerie, unnatural, and it makes the fisherman shift in his seat, acutely aware of the receding shoreline in front of him and the exposed emptiness at his back. The fisherman can count on one hand the number of times the cove’s ever been this still. He can’t see it from his seat in the boat, but the fisherman knows that to his left Jason’s Cape juts into the dark. Beyond that, the rapturous hum of the Pacific beckons him.
The little boat parts the dark saltwater-glass of moon and stars like a knife, leaving gentle ripples in her wake. They’ve been caught in a gentle current, left over from the tide’s turn. The fisherman rolls his salt-callused hands over the oars’ wood-damp handles. Now that the undercurrent has caught him, he’ll need them only to stop.


He’ll know the place.


Mary always said that the ocean called him. She would tell him that every time she found him on the porch, smoking a pipe, watching the water. “Listen to me!” she’d laugh, beckoning. “Come back to bed.” The fisherman would always laugh along with her, never saying a thing. His wife didn’t need to know she was right. The Call didn’t matter. No one else seemed to hear, and besides, there seemed more important things than heeding it. Like Mary, like his children, like making a living. But he’s old now, and his children are grown and gone and have families of their own, and since Mary’s passing the Call seems to have only gotten louder.
