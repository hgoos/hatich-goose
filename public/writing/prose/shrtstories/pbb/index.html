Plastic, Bone, Bisque

March 29, 2022

A note for those not familiar with clay:
*Clay can be virtually recycled throughout its lifetime in a studio, up until the point where it goes through its first firing in a kiln. Clay goes through three stages in studio: plastic (at its softest and most pliable), leather hard (still pliable but much stiffer and harder to work with), and bone-dry (virtually all the water inside the clay has evaporated and you are left with a rather fragile, brittle material).

At any point during these stages water can be added to the clay to revert it to its plastic stage. Once clay has gone through the first firing, however, you can no longer “recycle” it. This is because the structure of the clay molecules undergo a change in the kiln: the silica in the clay melting and hardening into a glass-like structure. The piece that comes out of the first firing is called “bisqueware,” which is extremely durable.*

Margaret was a potter. She worked with glazes and temperatures and clay. She worked with her hands. And she was good. Dammed good. She won awards for her pieces, functional or decorative, and she always took great pleasure in smashing those pieces whenever we got into it.

“Look what you made me do!” she would scream as hours of work became tiny, glazeware fragments on the floor, on the wall.
It was on one such occasion that I finally left.

Since we had been children, Margaret had been the potter and I had been the clay. She had molded me and re-shaped me to her liking until there was nothing of me left.

She had always been quick to put me in my place (“boys don’t cry”) and that night as I walked out the door, flattened and formless and aimless, I knew that I couldn’t go back.

I could hear her running after me, calling out, “Jim wait, come back! I’m sorry!” but I didn’t stop walking.

The Universe must’ve been on my side that night because at the moment I reached the stop on Denny and Main Street a bus pulled up right in front of me. I waved it down and hurried aboard but at the last second Margaret caught up and grabbed on to my sleeve.

“Please Jim!” she begged, “Don’t do this. I love you. I’m sorry.”
She was so sure I was bone-dry. That with a little water and time and sweet words I would become plastic again. She did not know that I had finally, finally, been fired.

“No,” I said, silica-structure in my voice. “I don’t think you are.” I tugged my sleeve out of her grip, sat down on the bus, and then sobbed as we drove down Main Street. You can’t make bisqueware plastic again, but you can sure as hell crack it.
