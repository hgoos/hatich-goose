<span style="margin-left:2.5em;">The beach glows tonight. It’s a soft glow, a murmuration of light.</span> 
    <span style="margin-left:2.5em;">It’s not anything spectacular, but everything has that moonshine sheen to it. The moon hangs there in the sky, a quarter past her zenith, like some silver-glass fishing float adrift in a midnight sea.</span>
<span style="margin-left:2.5em;">On the shoreline an old fisherman heaves a roughened wooden rowboat towards the water.</span> The keel of the boat gouges the sand as he drags it along, leaving behind a scar of shadow and silver. A few hours ago, the vessel had been bobbing in the surf with the pull of the moon and the will of the wind, knocking up against the knolls of beach grass some fifty yards back. Even if there had been a new moon, the summer squall that had overtaken the little cove would’ve been enough to push the tide line that high. The storm had been something fierce, cutting through the faulty shutters of the fisherman’s cottage, but the tumult had suddenly died, leaving the little inlet as still as could be. With a sudden slake give, the boat reaches the waterline, and the fisherman propels her forward, slotting right into her belly like he has done a thousand times before.
    <span style="margin-left:2.5em;">The water’s still tonight, even for a sudden clearing.</span> It’s eerie, unnatural, and it makes the fisherman shift in his seat, acutely aware of the receding shoreline in front of him and the exposed emptiness at his back. The fisherman can count on one hand the number of times the cove’s ever been this still. He can’t see it from his seat in the boat, but the fisherman knows that to his left Jason’s Cape juts into the dark. Beyond that, the rapturous hum of the Pacific beckons him.
<span style="margin-left:2.5em;">The little boat parts the dark saltwater-glass of moon and stars like a knife, leaving gentle ripples in her wake.</span> They’ve been caught in a gentle current, left over from the tide’s turn. The fisherman rolls his salt-callused hands over the oars’ wood-damp handles. Now that the undercurrent has caught him, he’ll need them only to stop.
<br>
    <span style="margin-left:2.5em;">He’ll know the place.</span>
    </br>
<span style="margin-left:2.5em;">Mary always said that the ocean called him.</span> She would tell him that every time she found him on the porch, smoking a pipe, watching the water. “Listen to me!” she’d laugh, beckoning. “Come back to bed.” The fisherman would always laugh along with her, never saying a thing. His wife didn’t need to know she was right. The Call didn’t matter. No one else seemed to hear, and besides, there seemed more important things than heeding it. Like Mary, like his children, like making a living. But he’s old now, and his children are grown and gone and have families of their own, and since Mary’s passing the Call seems to have only gotten louder.

<br>
    <span style="margin-left:2.53em;">The boat stops its drifting as the fisherman maneuvers his oars, extricating the craft from the current.</span>  They eddy a moment, the boat gently pivoting with the excess momentum, bringing the fisherman around starboard. The fisherman allows the boat to bob, taking in his surroundings as they come into view: the silhouettes of the bluffs that border the cove, pitch black against the deep dark blue of the midnight sky; the stark white column of Argo’s Lighthouse reaching up and up and up, its gaze ever bright and vigilant; the velvety reflection of the stars and moon lapping gently against the side of his little boat. The Pacific, which seemed so far away moments ago, draws near, filling his ears with her roar.
    </br>
<br>
    <span style="margin-left:2.5em;">This is the place.</span>
    </br> 

<span style="margin-left:2.5em;">The fisherman peers over the edge of the boat and catches his reflection in the dark glass of the water.</span> He sees his face so clearly, his skin imbued with the silver of the moon. The sand had looked so lovely in that light, and even the rotting pickets of his fence had been graced when clad in that silver luster. It looks wrong on him somehow. Twisted.
    <span style="margin-left:2.5em;">The fisherman has seen drowned bodies before.</span>
<span style="margin-left:2.5em;">Everything has stopped. Like the painting of the cresting wave hanging on the wall of his living room.</span> All the colors and shapes and light, all the movement and momentum, all of it---all of it---suspended, frozen for a single moment.
    <span style="margin-left:2.5em;">The Pacific rings in his ears, reverberating towards some ancient core that stumbled out of the deep waters, gasping for air.</span>
    
<br>
    <span style="margin-left:2.5em;">This is the place.
        </span>
    </br>

<br>
    <span style="margin-left:2.5em;">The water is cold.</span> 
    Colder than he expected. It must’ve been that squall earlier that night; it must’ve churned up all that deep cold water; it must’ve brought it up the surface. The fisherman gasps as the chill envelopes him, forcing air from his lungs. A dark thrill of fear settles over him as he watches his breath drift away from him, suspended as silver glass floats. He doesn’t fight the pull of the Deep. He doesn’t fight as his little boat’s shape becomes ever smaller, as the moon’s light becomes an ever-faint murmuration. Everything is sudden quiet now, in the dark, muffled and muted by the swaddle of sea and salt. Then he hears it. Faint at first, but louder it grows. A steady rhythm that makes his body thrum in time.
    </br>
<span style="margin-left:2.5em;"><em>The-Deep. The-Deep. The-Deep.</em></span>

<br>
    <span style="margin-left:2.5em;">It beckons.
